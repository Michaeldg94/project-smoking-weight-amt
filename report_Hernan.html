The Causal Effect of Smoking Cessation on Weight Gain¶

Michael Duarte Gonçalves, Mircea-Adrian Guinea, Tina Sikharulidze

March 2026

Abstract¶

We estimate the average causal effect of smoking cessation on body weight change using data from 1566 cigarette smokers in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey Epidemiologic Follow-up Study (NHEFS). Individuals who quit smoking between the 1971 baseline and the 1982 follow-up gained more weight than those who continued. We adjust for nine baseline confounders under exchangeability, positivity, consistency, and SUTVA. Manski bounds computed without the ignorability assumption span a wide interval, illustrating the identifying power of this assumption. We apply nine estimation strategies: inverse probability weighting (stabilised, nonstabilised, and trimmed), propensity score matching and stratification, the parametric g-formula, augmented IPW (doubly robust), and Double LASSO. All methods yield consistent estimates of approximately 3.4 to 3.5 kg (95% CIs overlapping), suggesting that quitting smoking causes a clinically meaningful weight gain over a decade. Conditional average treatment effects by sex and baseline BMI show the effect is present across all subgroups.

Introduction¶

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey Data I Epidemiologic Follow-up Study (NHEFS) is a longitudinal study jointly initiated by the National Center for Health Statistics and the National Institute on Aging. Participants completed a baseline visit between 1971 and 1975 and a follow-up visit around 1982. The dataset we analyse is a subset of 1566 cigarette smokers aged 25 to 74 at baseline who had complete information on key variables at both visits (Hernan & Robins, 2021, p. 149).

Our causal question is: what is the average causal effect of smoking cessation on body weight change over the roughly ten-year follow-up period? The treatment variable A is coded 1 if the individual reported having quit smoking before the 1982 visit and 0 otherwise. The outcome Y is weight in 1982 minus weight in 1971, measured in kilograms.

Of the 1566 individuals, 403 (25.7%) reported quitting smoking. Quitters gained on average 4.5 kg, while non-quitters gained 2.0 kg. The unadjusted difference of 2.5 kg cannot be interpreted causally because quitters and non-quitters differ systematically in baseline characteristics that also affect weight change. Quitters were on average four years older, more likely to be white, heavier at baseline, and smoked fewer cigarettes per day (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 149-150).

To move from association to causation we need to adjust for confounding. Following Hernan and Robins (2021, p. 150), we assume that nine baseline covariates are sufficient to block all backdoor paths between treatment and outcome:

Under the assumptions of exchangeability, positivity, and consistency, the average causal effect is identified from the observed data. Our causal directed acyclic graph posits that the confounders L cause both treatment A and outcome Y, while treatment does not cause any component of L (since L is measured at baseline). Under conditional exchangeability given L, the backdoor path from A to Y through L is blocked by adjustment, and the remaining association between A and Y reflects the causal effect (Hernan & Robins, 2021, Chapters 6-7).

We estimate this causal effect using two complementary methods: inverse probability (IP) weighting (Chapter 12) and the parametric g-formula via standardization (Chapter 13). Both methods rely on the same identification assumptions but place parametric restrictions on different parts of the data distribution, providing a useful robustness check.

The DAG encodes three sets of edges. First, L causes A: baseline characteristics such as age, sex, smoking intensity, and weight influence whether a person quits smoking. Second, L causes Y: the same characteristics affect weight change independently of quitting (e.g., heavier individuals may gain less weight due to regression to the mean, and older smokers may have different metabolic trajectories). Third, A causes Y: smoking cessation itself alters metabolism and appetite, leading to weight gain. There is no edge from A to L because L is measured at baseline before the treatment decision.

We apply the d-separation criterion to verify that L is a valid adjustment set (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 73-76). The only non-causal (backdoor) path from A to Y is A ← L → Y. Conditioning on L blocks this path because L is a non-collider on it. After conditioning, the only remaining open path is the direct causal path A → Y, so the association in the conditional distribution P(Y | A, L) equals the causal effect.

We explicitly check for collider bias. A collider is a variable caused by two or more variables on a path. In our DAG, no variable on the backdoor path A ← L → Y is a collider, so conditioning on L does not open any spurious path. A potential concern would arise if we conditioned on a post-treatment variable (e.g., a health outcome measured between baseline and follow-up) that is caused by both A and Y, as this would be a collider and conditioning on it would introduce non-causal association between A and Y (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 73-78). We avoid this by restricting our adjustment set to pre-treatment covariates.

Identification assumptions¶

Before estimation we state the four identification assumptions required to give the adjusted association a causal interpretation.

Exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding) requires that, conditional on the nine measured confounders L, the potential outcomes are independent of treatment assignment. This assumption is untestable; its plausibility rests on domain knowledge that the nine covariates capture the major shared causes of smoking cessation and weight change measured in the NHEFS (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 27-30).

Positivity requires that, for every stratum of confounders with nonzero probability, both treatment levels are observed: if Pr(L = l) > 0 then 0 < Pr(A = 1 | L = l) < 1. With continuous covariates we check this empirically via the propensity score overlap (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 30-31).

Consistency states that the observed outcome for an individual who received treatment level a equals their potential outcome under that treatment. This requires that the treatment is well-defined, which is a simplification since "quitting smoking" can happen through different mechanisms (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 31-35).

The stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) requires that the potential outcome for individual i depends only on their own treatment assignment, not on the treatment received by other individuals. In the NHEFS context, SUTVA is plausible because smoking cessation is largely an individual decision and the survey sampled participants from across the United States with no clustering that would generate direct interference. However, household-level spillovers are conceivable: if a spouse quits smoking, this might change the household food environment or encourage the partner to also quit, creating indirect effects. Given the large and geographically dispersed sample, we treat such interference as negligible (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 3-4).

Manski bounds¶

Before imposing any assumptions beyond consistency and SUTVA, we can compute worst-case bounds on the average treatment effect following the partial identification approach of Manski. Without the exchangeability assumption, we can only bound E[Y(a)] by considering the best and worst cases for the missing potential outcomes. For continuous Y bounded in [y_min, y_max], the bounds on the ATE are (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 27-28):

$$\text{ATE} \in \big[\, E[Y|A=1]P(A=1) + y_{\min}P(A=0) - E[Y|A=0]P(A=0) - y_{\max}P(A=1),$$ $$E[Y|A=1]P(A=1) + y_{\max}P(A=0) - E[Y|A=0]P(A=0) - y_{\min}P(A=1) \,\big]$$

These bounds are wide because we make no assumptions about the distribution of counterfactual outcomes for the untreated or the treated. The width illustrates how much identifying power the exchangeability assumption provides.

IP weighting¶

We start with inverse probability (IP) weighting. The idea is simple: instead of modelling the outcome, we model the probability of receiving treatment (the propensity score) and reweight observations so that treatment becomes independent of confounders. This mimics what a randomised experiment would do -- if we know why some people quit and others did not, we can undo that selection by giving more weight to individuals who were unlikely to receive their actual treatment (Hernan & Robins, 2021, p. 151).

We use stabilised weights SW = f(A) / f(A|L), which have mean 1 and are less variable than the nonstabilised weights 1/f(A|L) (Hernan & Robins, 2021, p. 153). We also report nonstabilised weights to verify that the point estimate does not change, as expected from theory.

p_treated = nhefs[TREATMENT].mean()

# Stabilised weights: SW = f(A) / f(A|L)
nhefs["sw"] = np.where(

    p_treated / nhefs["ps"],
    (1 - p_treated) / (1 - nhefs["ps"]),

Weight trimming sensitivity¶

Extreme IP weights can inflate the variance of the estimator. We test sensitivity by trimming the stabilised weights at the 1st and 99th percentiles: any weight below the 1st percentile is set to the 1st percentile value, and any weight above the 99th percentile is set to the 99th percentile value. We then refit the MSM and compare the estimate and confidence interval to the untrimmed version (Hernan & Robins, 2021, p. 155).

Propensity score matching¶

As an alternative to IP weighting, we estimate the effect using nearest-neighbour propensity score matching with replacement. For each treated individual, we find the control individual with the closest propensity score and compute the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) as the mean difference in outcomes between matched pairs. Matching with replacement allows each control to serve as a match for multiple treated individuals, which reduces bias at the cost of increased variance (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 188-189).

Parametric g-formula (standardization)¶

IP weighting models the treatment mechanism; the g-formula takes the opposite approach and models the outcome directly. We fit a regression of weight change on treatment and confounders, then predict each person's outcome under "everyone quits" and "nobody quits." The difference in means is the ATE (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 163-164).

We use the g-formula as our second estimator because it relies on different assumptions from IP weighting. If one model is wrong but the other is right, at least one estimate will be consistent. Getting similar numbers from both methods is reassuring -- it suggests the result does not hinge on any one modelling choice.

The outcome model follows Program 13.3 (Hernan & Robins, 2021, p. 165): all nine confounders, quadratic terms for continuous covariates, and a product term between treatment and smoking intensity.

Double LASSO¶

IP weighting and the g-formula both require us to pick a functional form for the nuisance models. If we get that form wrong, the ATE estimate can be biased. The Double LASSO lets the data decide which covariates and transformations matter, using regularisation to shrink unimportant terms toward zero.

We set up a partially linear model: Y = alpha * D + g(W) + epsilon, where alpha is the treatment effect and g(W) captures the confounders. The trick is that running a single LASSO of Y on (D, W) would shrink alpha toward zero, which we do not want. The Double LASSO avoids this by partialling out W from both sides: (1) LASSO of Y on W to get residuals Y_tilde, (2) LASSO of D on W to get residuals D_tilde, (3) OLS of Y_tilde on D_tilde to estimate alpha. Step (2) matters: without it, confounding persists even after step (1). We use HC1 robust standard errors for inference.

lasso_y = LassoCV(cv=5, random_state=42, max_iter=10000).fit(W_scaled, Y_vec)
Y_tilde = Y_vec - lasso_y.predict(W_scaled)

# Step 2: LASSO of D on W
lasso_d = LassoCV(cv=5, random_state=42, max_iter=10000).fit(W_scaled, D_vec)
D_tilde = D_vec - lasso_d.predict(W_scaled)

# Step 3: OLS of Y_tilde on D_tilde
alpha_hat = np.sum(D_tilde * Y_tilde) / np.sum(D_tilde ** 2)

# HC1 robust standard error
n = len(Y_vec)
e_hat = Y_tilde - alpha_hat * D_tilde
hc1_var = (n / (n - 1)) * np.sum(D_tilde ** 2 * e_hat ** 2) / (np.sum(D_tilde ** 2)) ** 2
hc1_se = np.sqrt(hc1_var)
ci_dlasso = (alpha_hat - 1.96 * hc1_se, alpha_hat + 1.96 * hc1_se)

Augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW)¶

Hernan and Robins discuss IP weighting and the g-formula as separate strategies, but there is a natural way to combine them: augmented IPW, also known as doubly robust estimation. We start with the IP weighted estimator and add a correction term based on the outcome model. If either model -- the propensity score or the outcome regression -- is correctly specified, the AIPW estimator is consistent. We only get the wrong answer if both models are wrong at the same time. As the book puts it, "doubly robust estimators give us two chances to get it right" (Hernan & Robins, 2021, Fine Point 13.2, p. 167).

The AIPW estimator for the ATE is:

$$\hat{\tau}_{AIPW} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ \hat{\mu}(1, W_i) - \hat{\mu}(0, W_i) + \frac{D_i (Y_i - \hat{\mu}(1, W_i))}{\hat{e}(W_i)} - \frac{(1 - D_i)(Y_i - \hat{\mu}(0, W_i))}{1 - \hat{e}(W_i)} \right]$$

where mu-hat is the outcome model and e-hat is the propensity score. The advantage over plain IPW or g-formula alone is the double robustness property.

Instrumental variable candidates¶

The methods used so far assume all confounders are measured. Instrumental variable (IV) estimation can identify causal effects without that assumption, provided a valid instrument Z exists. Chapter 16 of Hernan and Robins (2021, p. 193) defines three conditions: (i) Z is associated with treatment A (relevance), (ii) Z affects the outcome Y only through A (exclusion restriction), and (iii) Z and Y share no common causes (marginal exchangeability).

Hernan and Robins (2021, p. 194) propose cigarette price as a candidate instrument for smoking cessation. Higher prices discourage smoking (relevance), plausibly affect weight only through smoking behaviour (exclusion), and state-level prices are unlikely to share causes with individual weight change (independence). Fine Point 16.1 (p. 195) classifies this as an "access" instrument.

The NHEFS dataset contains seven tobacco-policy variables at the state level: price71, price82, price71_82, tax71, tax82, tax71_82, and hightax82. Following the book, we use hightax82 as the primary binary instrument and test all candidates for relevance. Only condition (i) can be checked empirically; conditions (ii) and (iii) rest on substantive arguments (Hernan & Robins, 2021, p. 195).

As the book notes, "the compatibility of estimates across methods is reassuring because each method's estimate is based on different modeling assumptions" (p. 168). IP weighting models the treatment side, the g-formula models the outcome side, AIPW combines both for double robustness, and the Double LASSO lets the data select functional forms. Discussion¶

We estimated the average causal effect of smoking cessation on weight change using nine methods applied to NHEFS data from 1566 cigarette smokers. All methods produce estimates around 3.4 to 3.5 kg with overlapping confidence intervals, consistent with Hernan and Robins (2021, Chapters 12-13). Quitting smoking causes a real and clinically meaningful weight gain over a decade.

Why use so many methods? Because each one can fail differently. IP weighting needs the propensity score model to be right; the g-formula needs the outcome model; the AIPW estimator only needs one of them to be right; and the Double LASSO lets the data select functional forms. The fact that they all agree tells us the result does not depend on any one modelling choice.

The Manski bounds showed that without the exchangeability assumption, the data alone tell us almost nothing. The dramatic narrowing from bounds spanning tens of kilograms to point estimates around 3.5 kg is entirely driven by the assumption of no unmeasured confounding given the nine covariates.

We considered but did not implement two additional methods from Hernan and Robins. G-estimation of structural nested models (Chapter 14, p. 171) is designed for settings with time-varying treatments and adds little when treatment is point-identified as here. Instrumental variables (Chapter 16, p. 193) would require a valid instrument; the NHEFS dataset contains cigarette price variables (price71, price82), but these are likely weak instruments for quitting -- price changes between 1971 and 1982 were small relative to the variation in quitting behaviour, and the exclusion restriction (price affects weight only through quitting) is hard to defend given that price may correlate with socioeconomic factors that independently affect weight. We therefore did not pursue IV estimation.

The CATE analysis reveals the treatment effect is present across all sex and BMI subgroups, with some variation but widely overlapping confidence intervals.

There are several limitations. First, we assume conditional exchangeability given nine measured confounders. Unmeasured confounders like motivation to change health behaviours or dietary changes could bias the results. The E-value suggests a moderately strong unmeasured confounder would be needed to explain away the effect, but we cannot rule it out. Second, we restricted the analysis to the 1566 individuals with observed outcomes, dropping 63 who were censored. If quitting affects whether someone is lost to follow-up, this introduces selection bias (Hernan & Robins, 2021, pp. 158-159). Third, all methods share the fundamental reliance on conditional ignorability, which we cannot test from the data.

For future work, censoring weights could formally address the missing data. Causal forests could reveal finer treatment effect heterogeneity. And sensitivity analysis methods beyond the E-value could more precisely quantify the impact of specific hypothetical unmeasured confounders.
